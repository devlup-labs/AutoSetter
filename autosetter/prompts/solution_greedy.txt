The objective is not to find the globally correct algorithm. The objective is to construct a solution that represents a realistic algorithmic failure: it should be syntactically correct, structurally credible, computationally plausible, and likely to pass simple or sample tests while failing hidden cases because of a fundamental but subtle algorithmic assumption.

PHASE 2 — SELECT A PLAUSIBLE FALLACY
====================================

Construct exactly one primary flawed assumption or heuristic model.

The chosen fallacy must be:

* Mathematically plausible.
* Easy for a reasonable contestant to believe.
* Consistent throughout the implementation.
* Strong enough to produce Wrong Answer on some valid cases.
* Not obviously absurd.
* Preferably aligned with a common competitive-programming mistake.

Use the problem structure to select an appropriate failure pattern.

Possible failure transformations include:

1. LOCAL-OPTIMUM SUBSTITUTION

If the correct problem requires global optimization, replace it with a local greedy decision.

Typical forms:

* Choose the largest immediate gain.
* Choose the smallest immediate cost.
* Choose the earliest finishing item.
* Choose the locally best transition.
* Commit permanently without considering future states.

Required weakness:
The locally optimal decision must not be guaranteed to imply a globally optimal solution.

---

2. DYNAMIC-PROGRAMMING COLLAPSE

If the correct solution requires maintaining multiple states:

* Keep only one state.
* Select only the currently best state.
* Discard alternative states.
* Replace recurrence exploration with a greedy choice.

Required weakness:
Different future outcomes can depend on states that were discarded.

---

3. TREE ASSUMPTION ON A GENERAL GRAPH

If the input permits a general graph:

* Traverse using parent-child logic.
* Ignore revisits or alternate paths.
* Treat the first traversal structure as sufficient.
* Assume a unique path where multiple paths may exist.

Required weakness:
Cycles, alternate paths, or cross edges can invalidate the result.

---

4. ADJACENCY-ONLY REDUCTION

If the correct problem requires considering arbitrary pairs:

* Sort by a convenient key.
* Compare only adjacent elements.

Required weakness:
The optimal or valid pair may be non-adjacent under that ordering.

---

5. SINGLE-CRITERION REDUCTION

Reduce a multi-objective or constrained decision to one metric.

Examples:

* Sort only by value.
* Sort only by ratio.
* Optimize cost while ignoring feasibility interactions.
* Optimize time while ignoring downstream constraints.

Required weakness:
The ignored dimension can change the correct global answer.

---

6. INDEPENDENCE ASSUMPTION

Treat dependent decisions as independent.

Examples:

* Optimize each component separately.
* Sum local optima.
* Make choices without accounting for shared resources.
* Assume pairwise decisions do not affect later feasibility.

Required weakness:
Interactions invalidate the composition of local answers.

---

7. MONOTONICITY ASSUMPTION

Assume that an intuitive ordering property always holds.

Examples:

* More is always better.
* Earlier is always better.
* Smaller prefix implies smaller final answer.
* A sorted decision boundary is sufficient.

Required weakness:
The problem admits non-monotonic counterexamples.

---

8. ONE-PASS DECISION

Use a single forward or backward pass when revisiting decisions may be necessary.

Required weakness:
Later information can invalidate earlier commitments.

---

9. REPRESENTATIVE-CASE ASSUMPTION

Replace a complete search or state space with one representative candidate.

Examples:

* Test only the first occurrence.
* Keep only the maximum/minimum representative.
* Select one component representative.
* Check only one endpoint.

Required weakness:
Other candidates may produce the correct or optimal result.

---

10. APPROXIMATE STRUCTURAL REDUCTION

Simplify the original structure.

Examples:

* Convert graph behavior into linear behavior.
* Treat intervals independently.
* Treat a cyclic structure as acyclic.
* Replace a set of constraints with a simpler sufficient-looking subset.

Required weakness:
The omitted structure matters for correctness.
